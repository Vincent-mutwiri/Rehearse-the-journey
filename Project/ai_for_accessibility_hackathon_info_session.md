## Building AI That Adapts to People with Disabilities

* **What it is:** A one-day hackathon where makers and co-designers build AI tools that adapt websites, apps and devices to each person’s needs.
* **Who is behind it:** The AI for Accessibility Collective, with the Stanford Institute for Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence (HAI), MIT Media Lab, the University of Washington and UCL’s Global Disability Innovation Hub, funded by Google.org.
* **Our part:** Localization. Senses Hub runs the Nairobi hackathon, built around Kenyan problems and the open-source AI for Accessibility Toolkit.

## The Toolkit in One Slide

* **Need:** The person says what they need, in their own words
* **Profile:** Stored on their device, shared only with their permission
* **Skill:** Which adaptations fit, and at what settings
* **Adaptation:** The app changes, and the person approves, corrects or undoes it

> About 50 ready-made adapters to build on. Node 20 or later. A research prototype: expect rough edges, and log what breaks as toolkit issues.

### Problem Statement 1 of 6 · Blind and low vision
#### Sheng Mode for Screen Readers

> How might we adapt a Kenyan web page so that a blind or low-vision user can listen to mixed English, Swahili, and Sheng content in their chosen way, without having to hand the phone to another person?

* **The problem:** Blind and low-vision users who rely on TalkBack can encounter Kenyan web pages that switch between English, Swahili, and Sheng. A screen reader set for one language may pronounce mixed-language text poorly or skip parts, reducing independent access to notices, banking information, and other web content.
* **Co-designer:** A blind or low-vision person who uses TalkBack on an Android phone

---

### Problem Statement 2 of 6 · Blind and low vision
#### Pay Without Sight

> How might we help a blind user complete a realistic government-service form through an accessible, step-by-step web flow, while ensuring that submission and payment happen only after the user explicitly confirms?

* **The problem:** A blind user may be unable to complete a multi-step government-service form independently when fields, one-time codes, payment prompts, or receipts are not accessible. Asking another person to help can expose private information, including payment details or a PIN.
* **Co-designer:** A blind screen-reader user with experience of online government services or mobile money

---

### Problem Statement 3 of 6 · Deaf and hard of hearing
#### An Agent That Keeps Watch

> How might we let a person define a small set of important phone events and receive a clear alert in their chosen format, while keeping all rules and message content private and under their control?

* **The problem:** Default phone alerts may not make important events clear or distinguishable for a person who is blind, Deaf, or hard of hearing. Too many low-priority alerts can also make it harder to notice the messages or events the person has chosen to prioritise.
* **Co-designer:** A Deaf or hard of hearing smartphone user, with a KSL interpreter on the day

---

### Problem Statement 4 of 6 · Physical and motor
#### A Voice That Understands Me

> How might we let a person whose speech is not recognised by standard voice control use a small set of everyday app commands, in their preferred language, without the system guessing what they meant?

* **The problem:** Standard voice-control systems may not understand a person with a speech impairment. When voice commands fail, the person may be unable to operate app controls independently or may have to rely on another person.
* **Co-designer:** A person with a speech impairment, for example a person with cerebral palsy

---

### Problem Statement 5 of 6 · Physical and motor
#### Game Night

> How might we adapt one open-source browser game so people with different access needs can play together using the controls, pace, and feedback that work for them?

* **The problem:** Browser games often assume that players can see the screen, use a mouse, react quickly, and tolerate continuous play without pauses. These assumptions can exclude blind players and people with motor or other access needs from playing with friends or family.
* **Co-designer:** A person with a motor disability, such as limited hand use, who enjoys games

---

### Problem Statement 6 of 6 · Cognitive and psychosocial
#### Rehearse the Journey

> How might we adapt a short virtual rehearsal of a Nairobi journey or service visit to a person’s access preferences, so they can practise at their own pace and exit whenever they choose?

* **The problem:** Some people find a journey to a matatu stage, hospital reception, or another public service confusing, stressful, or difficult to plan. They may benefit from learning the steps in advance and controlling the pace and sensory demands of the practice experience.
* **Co-designer:** A person with a cognitive or psychosocial disability who finds such journeys stressful

## Working with Co-designers

### Co-designers Are Full Team Members
* Share the decisions: they help define the problem and decide what gets built
* Speak to the person, not to their interpreter, guide or companion
* Introduce yourself to blind participants each time you speak to them
* Ask before helping, and accept no as an answer
* Never touch a wheelchair, cane, guide dog or device without permission
* Respect privacy: do not ask about anyone’s disability unless they raise it

### Our Code of Conduct
* Be respectful, honest and kind, in the room and online
* No harassment, discrimination or jokes about disability; no alcohol or drugs
* Use made-up test profiles, never real personal or payment data
* Respect each person’s photo and video consent
* Your team, co-designers included, owns what it builds
